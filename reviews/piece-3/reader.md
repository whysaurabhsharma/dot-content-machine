## 1. Three places I'd stop reading

**Line 27: "the selector switched after question 1. Nine of its ten answers ran in Balanced."**
This is where the test falls apart. The headline compares Economy with Balanced, but the Balanced side is a batch that was supposed to be Economy and switched by accident. Nobody designed that comparison. Once I learn the control batch was an accident, I stop trusting the headline. The opening summary (line 3) says "Economy didn't save credits over Balanced" and never mentions this.

**Lines 56 and 87: the dollar figure contradicts itself.**
Line 56 says "We found no Dot page that publishes a dollar price for a credit." Line 87 says $1 per credit matches "the $300 advertised at signup." So either there is a published dollar figure or there isn't. Also, the one number a Head of Data would want, $0.58 per answer, is labelled "Assumption, not confirmed by Dot" twice. If the dollar figure is unconfirmed, I can't take it to Finance, and that is the reason I opened the piece.

**Line 23 together with lines 68 to 74: correct answers were scored against the tool's own definition.**
Questions 8 and 9 were scored against the delivered-orders scope that the context note sets. The plain reading of "orders purchased in 2017" and "average order value" gives different answers. The draft admits that scoring on all orders would have marked four answers wrong. "10 of 10 correct" then reads as a matter of how you score. The draft makes a fair point that the answer named its scope, but a sceptical reader sees a vendor that marked its own homework.

The draft treats rounding as the only source of error. It isn't. Each mode ran once, batch 2 always ran second (line 82), and line 80 admits "the same question can take a different path on another day." Nobody measured how much the cost varies from one run to the next, so a 0.5-credit gap between two single runs tells me nothing. That makes the headline "Economy didn't save credits" unsupported, not just hedged.
